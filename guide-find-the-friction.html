Where Is the Friction in Your Business?

Look beyond the symptom and follow one piece of work from start to finish.

← Back to resources

Start With One Real Example

Choose something recent: a customer who waited, an estimate that stalled, or a task that returned to you three times. Describe what actually happened, rather than what the process was supposed to be. A specific example gives the discussion a useful starting point.

Write down where the work began, which people touched it, what information they needed, and how it reached the next step. Include the waiting and checking that happened between the obvious tasks.

Look at People, Systems, and Process

For people, ask whether someone clearly owned the work and had the capacity and information to complete it. For systems, ask where the information lived and whether the next person could find it. For process, ask whether the next action and the definition of complete were clear.

A missed call can involve all three. The team may be occupied, the message may sit in an isolated inbox, and nobody may own returning it. Buying another tool before understanding that path could leave the original gap in place.

Find the Point That Creates Repeated Work

Notice where someone asks for the same information, copies it between tools, waits for an answer, or sends the work back. Ask what caused that extra step. Sometimes an approval is necessary. Sometimes the approval exists because ownership or decision boundaries were never clear.

Choose one point to examine first. Consider customer impact, frequency, effort, and how much depends on the owner. You do not need to redesign the whole business to learn something useful.

Try One Clear Change

Agree who owns the next step, where the information belongs, and how you will know the work is complete. Explain the change to the people who use it. Review several real examples after it has been used.

Compare what happened with your starting example. Did the waiting decrease? Did the same questions return? Did a different problem appear? Keep what helps, adjust what does not, and use the evidence to choose the next step.

A useful place to start

Pick one recurring task. Record its trigger, owner, information, next step, and completion point. Circle the handoff that produces the most waiting or repeated work.
